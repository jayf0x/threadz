Threadz · direction report · 26 Sep 2026

Everyone draws graphs.
Writers need lines.

You said the product is whether someone can see, and make, a coherent path through separate notes. I agree, and I read that as a UI problem first and a data problem second. This report looks at how about thirty tools, research prototypes and games handle that path, what they share, where they break, and what Threadz should build.

What changed since the last answer

A note stays the unit. The path through notes is the product.

Last time I said the note is the basic unit and everything else is a view. That's still true for storage, but it's the wrong thing to lead with. A typewriter stores units just fine. What a typewriter can't do is show you that the paragraph you wrote in March and the one you wrote on a walk yesterday belong to the same line of thought, and let you follow that line.

Two things change because of it:

  • I'm reversing one recommendation. I said "one home thread per note, show it elsewhere through link previews." If the product is paths, a note has to be able to sit on several paths at once, the way a station sits on several metro lines. Heptabase, Are.na, Supernotes and Workflowy all landed on the same answer.
  • The name already says it. Ariadne's thread gets you through a labyrinth and back out. A Memex "trail" was a new linear sequence laid across unrelated microfilm frames. Luhmann's numbered slips (21/3d7a) were a predefined path through a box of cards. In each of these, the thread is a path through material that lives elsewhere, and Threadz can mean exactly that.

A thread is a line drawn through notes. A note can sit on many lines. The pool is every note that sits on none yet.

Landscape

Where does a connection live?

Every tool in this space answers that one question, usually without saying so. Where a connection is stored decides what you can see, what you can make on a phone, and what survives an export. There are six answers.

In the text

Roam · Obsidian · Logseq · Reflect · Bear · Threadz today

An inline [[link]]. The link is part of the prose, so it's portable and says where the connection is.

BreaksA link can't say why it exists, and you can only make one while writing, with a keyboard.

In membership

Are.na · Heptabase · Supernotes · Workflowy · Scrivener collections

The same item placed in several collections. Two notes are connected because they sit in the same place.

BreaksA collection is a bag. It says two notes are related, but not in what order or why.

In space

Scapple · Kinopio · Muse · Obsidian Canvas · LiquidText · Campfire

Where a card sits on a board, and arrows drawn between cards. Closeness itself means something.

BreaksYou can't query it, it's mostly desktop or iPad only, and the meaning is lost when the layout changes.

In structure

Tana supertags · Capacities objects · TheBrain · Novelcrafter codex

Typed objects with fields and named relations: parent, child, jump, "participant in". Precise and searchable.

BreaksYou have to set up a schema first. TheBrain has been niche for 25 years and Tana's learning curve is famous.

In similarity

Napkin · Smart Connections · Reor · Mem · Linus Lee's lenses

Computed from embeddings or tags. Needs no work from you and turns up surprises.

BreaksIt's the machine's guess, not your claim. Mem is the cautionary tale: when that guess is the only way in, people stop trusting search.

In sequence

Luhmann's Folgezettel · Memex trails · Scrivener binder · Plottr grid · Aeon narrative order

The order is the claim: this follows from that. It's the only kind of connection that is also a piece of writing.

BreaksAlmost nobody treats it as a connection type. Tools keep one fixed order (the binder) or none at all.

The last lane is the one that matters. Coherence is a property of sequences: a graph shows that A touches B, and a line shows that B follows from A. Writers turn graphs into lines for a living. The popular tools cover the first five lanes, and the only mature tools that take the sixth seriously are Scrivener and Aeon Timeline. Both are desktop apps for managing a manuscript, with no pool of loose notes and no phone story.

What they all have in common

  1. A capture inbox, whatever it's called: Drafts, Heptabase Inbox, Muse's iPhone inbox, Roam's daily page.
  2. A "linked from" list. Every serious tool shows what points at the note you're reading.
  3. A local view of connections that works, next to a global one that doesn't: Obsidian's local graph is used and its global graph is admired once.
  4. Search as the main way around once there are more than a few hundred notes (⌘O, ⌘K).
  5. A split between devices: the phone captures and reads, the desktop or iPad arranges. Heptabase mobile couldn't move cards or draw arrows for a long time, Campfire's relationship web isn't on mobile, Scapple has no mobile app, and Muse's iPhone app is an inbox.
  6. Stable IDs under everything, so a link survives a rename.

What sets each apart

  1. Which screen is home. Heptabase opens on a whiteboard, Roam on today's page, Are.na on a channel, Napkin on one thought with its neighbours around it, Tana on an outline, Scrivener on a binder. That default view is the product's personality.
  2. How big the unit is. A page (Obsidian), a block (Roam, Tana), a card (Heptabase, Supernotes) or an object (Capacities). Smaller units are easier to connect and harder to read as prose.
  3. Who does the connecting. The user (Scapple), a schema (Tana) or a model (Napkin). Tools do best when the machine suggests and the person decides.
  4. What leaves with you. Markdown (Obsidian, iA Writer), JSON Canvas (Obsidian), or nothing.
Field notes

What to take from each, and what to leave

For each tool: the specific mechanism, the part to copy, and the part to avoid. These are grouped by what they help with, not by product category.

Seeing a connection

research · 1965–

Xanadu: visible links, parallel documents

Ted Nelson called the core problem of hypertext "being able to see connections side by side": two documents next to each other with coloured bands linking the related passages.

Take a link that shows its destination in context: the other note together with its neighbours on its own line.

Leave the global web of permissions and micropayments.

web · notes

Andy Matuschak's stacked panes

Each link you follow opens as a new column to the right, so the path you took stays on screen. Obsidian later made this a built-in option.

Take on desktop, your route through the notes stays visible as you go.

Leave columns on a phone. There's no width for them.

web · essays

gwern.net recursive popups

A preview can contain links that open their own previews, as deep as you like. They chose non-modal pop-ins so the reader never loses the page underneath.

Take a peek can open another peek, and closing one steps back a level.

game · strategy

Crusader Kings III nested tooltips

Every highlighted concept, including every character name, opens a tooltip that you can lock and then hover into for the next one. This is hypertext in a game with thousands of entities.

Take every mention can be tapped. On touch this is easier than on desktop because a tap locks by default.

Leave hover-to-open timing. The Paradox forums are full of people losing a stack of tooltips to one stray mouse move.

pdf · research

LiquidText ink links

Draw a line between any two points and it becomes a live link, with a button at each end that brings back the other side. Excerpts keep a link back to the exact spot they came from.

Take a connection has two ends, and you can tap either one to go to the other.

notes · graph

Obsidian graph view

A dense hairball beyond a few hundred notes: "it only ever offers a shape." The local graph at depth one or two is the part people keep open. Its defenders use it to spot orphans and to see which drafts are still thinly connected.

Take show only the neighbourhood of the current note. Treat orphans and thinly connected notes as useful signals.

Leave the global force-directed graph.

One item in many places

curation

Are.na "connect"

A block lives in any number of channels. There are no likes, no favourites and no time-sorted feed: "there is no such thing as a 'like'… instead we have connections."

Take connecting is the main gesture, and the list of places a note belongs to is shown on the note.

visual pkm

Heptabase card ↔ whiteboards

A card sits on many whiteboards, and editing it updates it everywhere. The card's Info panel lists "which whiteboards, and where on them" it appears, and a click takes you to that spot.

Take a list on each note of "on these lines, at this position", where each entry takes you there.

cards

Supernotes multi-parent

A card can have several parents. Their community forum has a thread titled "why do cards appear multiple times in outline?"

Leave duplicates that look identical. A note shown in a second place needs a mark saying so.

outliner

Workflowy mirrors

Live copies of a bullet, created with (( or ⌘⇧M. They're drawn with a distinct bullet style so you can tell a mirror from the original.

Take a text trigger to place an existing note, and a visual mark on repeated notes.

writing

iA Writer content blocks

A line starting with /Section.txt embeds another file, and it exports to HTML, PDF and Word. They published the syntax as an open spec.

Take a line can be exported as plain markdown that pulls its notes in order.

Order as meaning

1945

Memex trails

Bush proposed "a new linear sequence of microfilm frames across any arbitrary sequence", with personal comments and side trails, which you could name and pass on to someone else.

Take the whole model: a named path with a short note at each step, and branches off it.

1950s–90s

Luhmann's Folgezettel

A follow-up slip is filed right behind the one it continues, and the numbering (1 → 1a → 1a1) records the branch. The numbers are a fixed route through the box, with cross-references as shortcuts.

Take the idea that "this continues that" is a different kind of link from "see also".

writing · desktop

Scrivener's three views

Binder, corkboard and outliner show the same documents. Reorder in one and the others follow. Scrivenings mode joins a folder's documents into one scrolling text.

Take a line can be read as one continuous text, laid out as cards, or listed as rows.

fiction · time

Aeon Timeline two orders

Chronological order always contains everything. Narrative order is opt-in, and an item can be in either or both, "so you can model more backstory than you intend to include."

Take the date something was written is always kept, and reading order is something you add on top.

fiction · plotting

Plottr grid

Plotlines are rows, chapters are columns, and scenes are cards at the crossings. It's a two-dimensional view of connections that is really a table, so it doesn't need a free canvas.

Take for a map, tracks laid out as rows are easier to draw than a free graph and still legible.

Structure that grows out of text

fiction · codex

Novelcrafter codex tracking

Entry names and aliases are "instantly recognized and linked as you type", underlined in the manuscript and counted in a mentions tracker. There's a per-entry switch and optional case sensitivity.

Take mentions detected without any model. This is Threadz's best feature that works fully offline.

research · Ink & Switch

Potluck

Plain text becomes structured through "searches" (patterns like {number} g) and annotations shown over the text. Because the state is text, undo comes free. What failed: changing a shared search broke every document that used it, and non-programmers struggled.

Take text stays the source of truth and structure is highlighted on top of it (/todo already works this way).

Leave patterns users write themselves. Detect names and aliases, and don't expose regex.

objects

Capacities

Typed objects. When a link sits in a property, the property's name becomes the label on the graph edge.

Take a label on a connection comes from what the user already wrote, not from a relation type they had to define.

graph · 1998–

TheBrain plex

The active thought sits in the middle, parents above, children below, jumps to the side. Click one and it moves to the centre. The same links are also listed under the note as plain text.

Take tap a neighbour to make it the current note. That's the "game-like navigation" you described.

Leave the requirement to decide parent vs child vs jump for every link.

outliner · power

Tana search nodes

A saved search can live inside a note and refer to its PARENT, so every #project automatically lists its own #tasks.

Take a character's line can show "every note that mentions me" without any manual linking.

Space, and what it costs

research · tablet

Capstone

A "shelf" that works as a visible clipboard holding several items across devices. Zooming in to peek was intuitive. Their finding: the DOM is "misaligned with game-like continuous worlds", and choosing a stylus tool remained unsolved.

Take a visible tray that holds several notes while you move around. That's the carry gesture below.

Leave an infinite canvas built on the web stack as your first map.

tablet · canvas

Muse

No chrome, 120 fps, boards nested inside boards with zoom. Users said very wide and very tall boards felt "wobbly" and disorienting. Their conclusion: "extending to phones and desktops presents both technical and UI design problems."

Take speed counts as a feature, and zoom should snap to fixed levels.

desktop

Scapple

"Every note is equal". Connections are optional and there's no central node. Made by the Scrivener team as a messy pre-writing space, and never released for iOS.

Take a loose area where notes don't have to be connected yet. That's the pool.

spec

JSON Canvas

Obsidian's open, MIT-licensed format: nodes with x, y, width and height; edges with fromSide, toSide, label and arrow ends; groups.

Take if a free-form canvas ever ships, export to this format and don't invent another.

ai · resurfacing

Napkin

The note you're on sits in the middle with a "swarm" of related notes around it. Reviewers call clicking through it "addictive". Each morning it resurfaces a few notes to swipe away or pin.

Take tapping to the next note is a pleasure in itself, and suggestions are small cards you keep or swipe away.

Leave connections based only on tag overlap.

capture

Drafts · Things 3

Drafts opens straight into a blank editor and you decide what to do with the text afterwards. In Things 3 you drag the + button to the exact spot where the new item should go.

Take capture goes to the pool with no choices to make, and placing a note is a drag to a specific spot between two others.

Games

Games that are about connecting clues

You mentioned War Thunder: a simplified control scheme on top of a deep simulation. Several games are built around making connections, and they've solved "show me what I know and what's still missing" better than any notes app.

exploration

Outer Wilds ship log

Two views of the same facts. Map mode groups them by place. Rumor mode shows them as cards with arrows for how one fact points to another. Entries you haven't finished are marked "there's more to explore here."

Take two modes, where it lives and what it leads to, plus visible loose ends. A /todo or an open question on a note is a "more to explore" marker on the map.

rpg

Disco Elysium thought cabinet

Ideas are "the loot from conversations". A thought appears as an orb that you accept or reject. Only a few slots can be active at a time, and working a thought through takes time.

Take AI suggestions appear as orbs you accept or dismiss, never as silent changes. A small number of "active lines" gives you focus.

fmv · mystery

Her Story

The whole game is a search box over interview clips. You piece the story together out of order, one query at a time. "If you can Google, you can play."

Take search works as navigation. Chronology doesn't have to be the structure for a story to hold together.

adventure

Point-and-click inventory

Pick up an item, carry it across rooms, use it on something. One thumb, no dragging across the whole screen, and it works through any number of screen changes.

Take this is how to make a connection on a phone. See Q3.

The model

Lines, stops and the pool

A metro map is the one kind of network diagram most people read without training. It's legible because each line has one route, and the interesting points are where lines cross. That's the same shape as a writer's material: several lines of thought, and a few notes where they meet.

What this means for the data

The note (today's message: id, content, edit history) doesn't change. What's new is a stop: one small record saying that note N is on line L at position P. Today every message is exactly one stop on its thread, at position seq, so moving to this model is a backfill that doesn't change anything the user sees yet.

  • The pool is every note with no stops. This needs no new concept or new screen logic.
  • Branching, which inspiration.md parks as "hard, needs parent pointers", comes for free. A branch is a new line that shares the first N stops. Edits to shared notes show up on both lines because it's the same note. "Clone from here" stops making copies.
  • Links stay as they are ([[ ]] in the text, stored by id). A link made with a gesture instead of typing gets a small link record, with an optional one-line reason. That's the same split you already have for todos: a /todo in the text or a meta.todo flag.
  • Order within a line is chosen by the user. seq becomes "when it was written", and a stop's position becomes "where it goes in this line".
Hard questions

The nine decisions that shape everything else

I've skipped the easy ones (search, themes, export). Each question below has the evidence, the choice, my recommendation, and what's still open.

Q1

What is a connection, exactly?

Evidence

Inline links are portable but can't say why they exist. Drawn arrows carry meaning but disappear on export. Typed relations are precise but need a schema up front. Capacities labels a graph edge with the name of the property the link sits in. Are.na: "connections are paths we lay out for each other."

Choice

Links in the text only, link records only, positions on a canvas, or typed relations.

Recommendation. Two kinds, and nothing else stores meaning. "Follows from" is being next to each other on a line (a stop). "Relates to" is a link, either inline or a record, with an optional reason written in plain words ("father of", "contradicts", "same smell as ch. 1"). A canvas position is view state and syncs as layout, not meaning. A model's similarity score is a suggestion until you accept it.

Still open. Whether reasons that get reused turn into kinds. If you write "father of" five times, the app could offer it as a label, and an optional reverse label ("child of") on top of that. Letting labels harden into kinds only after you've used them keeps TheBrain's precision without its setup cost.

Q2

How do you see connections without a hairball?

Evidence

Global graphs fail (Obsidian). The views that work all show one neighbourhood: the local graph, TheBrain's plex, Napkin's swarm, Heptabase's "appears on" list, Roam's linked references. Xanadu, Matuschak, gwern and CK3 all show the connected thing in context, without navigating away.

Choice

One big graph screen, or connections shown at the place you're already reading.

Recommendation. Three distances, each one tap further than the last, all built around the note you're on.

  1. In the text. The gutter you already have (the todo checkbox column) gets a coloured dot for each other line the note is on, and a small count of incoming links. Mentions are underlined the way Novelcrafter does it.
  2. Beside the text. Tap a dot to get a peek: the other note with its previous and next stops on that line (Xanadu's parallel documents). A peek can open another peek. On desktop, peeks stack as columns (Matuschak).
  3. The station board. A screen for one note: every line through it with the previous and next stop on each, then links in, links out, and mentions. Tap a stop to go there. On a phone this replaces the map.
Q3

How do you make a connection with one thumb?

Evidence

No product has solved this. Heptabase mobile couldn't draw arrows, Campfire's relationship web isn't on mobile, and Muse handed the phone an inbox. What does work on phones: typing a trigger ([[, ((), Things' drag-to-a-spot, Napkin's tap-to-recentre, Capstone's visible shelf.

Choice

Typing only, a miniature canvas, or a new gesture.

Recommendation: carry. Long-press a note to pick it up. It docks as a chip above the tab bar and stays there while you move around the app (the point-and-click inventory, Capstone's shelf). Drop it between two stops to place it on that line, or on a note to link the two. Once it lands, an optional "why?" field appears, pre-filled with a suggestion from main when one is available. You can carry several notes at once. It works offline and needs no canvas. On desktop the same action is drag and drop, and typing [[ stays for linking while you write.

Q4

Where does time go?

Evidence

Roam and Logseq organise everything by day. Are.na refuses to sort by time. Aeon keeps chronological order for everything and makes narrative order opt-in. Her Story tells a coherent story entirely out of order. Threadz today: seq is the order within a thread and the only one.

Choice

This is what "neutral towards time" means in practice. There are three different clocks, and today's app only has the first.

Recommendation. The time a note was written is always kept and always available as a sort, but it never decides structure. A line's order is whatever the user chose. The pool is sorted by when notes were written, because it's an inbox and that's honest there. "Resurfacing" changes from "old" to "unconnected for a while": the note that's been in the pool longest, or a line nobody has added to in a while.

Still open. Story time is worth adding only if the target is fiction. It's a date or chapter field per stop, and it would give you Aeon's contradiction checks ("she's 11 here and 13 two scenes earlier").

Q5

One home per note, or many?

Evidence

Every tool that takes connecting seriously lets an item sit in many places: Are.na, Heptabase, Supernotes, Workflowy, Scrivener collections. The cost shows up as confusion (Supernotes' "why does this appear twice?") and as a harder sync.

Recommendation. Many. Keep an origin (the line where you wrote it) for provenance and default navigation, and show a mark on any note that's on other lines too (the gutter dots). Removing a note from a line removes the stop. Deleting the note warns first ("on 3 lines"). Stops merge by id with a removal marker, exactly like messages do today, so union merge still holds.

Q6

What is the AI's job?

Evidence

Napkin's automatic connections are fun to browse but built on shallow tags. Smart Connections surfaces related notes while you write. Mem's AI-only search lost people's trust. Linus Lee's work treats models as lenses: ways to look at your notes that turn up surprises. Your stance: main does the heavy lifting, the phone shows precomputed results.

Recommendation. Four jobs, all on main, all shown as orbs you accept or dismiss, all in the derived layer that never causes a merge conflict.

  • Scout: suggests a line, and a position, for a note in the pool.
  • Surveyor: proposes a new line when several pool notes cluster together.
  • Scribe: drafts the reason for a connection you just made.
  • Editor: reads a line in order and flags where the jump between two stops isn't explained. No graph tool can do this, because it only makes sense for sequences. It's the one AI feature that follows directly from "coherent paths".
Q7

Is a canvas needed?

Evidence

Capstone: the web DOM fights continuous spatial worlds. Muse: big boards feel wobbly, and phones are unsolved. Plottr and Aeon get two-dimensional views out of grids, with no free placement.

Recommendation. Build a computed map first and a free canvas later. The first map is a tracks view on desktop: each line is a row, positions run left to right, and shared notes are drawn as vertical connectors between rows (Plottr's grid combined with a subway diagram). Nothing is placed by hand, so there's nothing to author on a phone and nothing to sync but data. A free-form "desk" canvas can come afterwards as a separate view, desktop only, with positions as layout state and export to JSON Canvas.

Still open. Real metro-map layout (lines bent only at 45°) is a research problem. Tracks avoid it. Try the harder layout only if tracks feel too flat.

Q8

How much structure, and who adds it?

Evidence

Tana and Capacities: power comes from typing things up front. Potluck: structure should grow gradually out of the text, and user-written patterns don't scale. Novelcrafter: names and aliases alone get writers most of the way.

Recommendation. One piece of structure only: any line can be marked as being about something (a character, a place, an idea), with aliases. Every note that mentions one of those names gets underlined and shows up under that line. That's all done on the device with text matching, fully offline. No schemas, no fields, no user-written regex. Labels on connections can harden into kinds later (Q1).

What we need for sure, and what's still open

Needed for sure

  • Stops: a note on many lines, in an order you choose
  • The pool, as notes with no stops (capture lands here)
  • Carry: place and link with one thumb, offline
  • Gutter dots and incoming-link counts in the text
  • Peek that can open further peeks (columns on desktop)
  • Station board as the phone's map
  • Links you can follow in both directions (you already store them by id)
  • Mentions via "about" lines and aliases (offline)
  • Read a line as one continuous text, and export it as markdown
  • Search as navigation (⌘K and full-text search already exist)

Open

  • Reasons on links: optional (my pick) or encouraged?
  • When labels harden into kinds, and whether they get reverse labels
  • Story time: only if the target is fiction
  • Tracks view vs a real metro layout vs a free canvas desk
  • Which AI job ships first (I'd pick Scout)
  • Merge annotations into link reasons, or keep them separate
  • How many "active lines" are pinned for focus (Disco Elysium's slots)
  • Name: "thread" or "line" in the UI
Method

How to decide on features from now on

Six questions to run every idea through, before and during the build:

  1. Which distance does it serve? In the text, beside it, or the board/map. If it serves none, it isn't helping anyone see connections, so it needs its own justification (the Todos tab has one, most things won't).
  2. Can you do it with one thumb, offline? If making it needs a desktop, say so up front and give the phone a way to read it.
  3. Is it meaning or layout? Meaning (text, stops, links) syncs and merges. Layout (positions, zoom, open peeks) stays on the device.
  4. Does it survive as markdown? A line exported to markdown has to read correctly on its own.
  5. Does the AI suggest or decide? Suggest, as an orb you can see and undo. Always.
  6. Which clock does it use, and did the user pick it? Time-of-writing as the default structure is what makes the app feel like a chat log.

Build order

Each step can be shipped and used on its own, and each one teaches you something before the next.

  1. Stops, backfilled.One stop per existing message, position taken from seq. Nothing visible changes, and it proves sync, hashing and deep links still work. Then add drag-to-reorder within a thread.
  2. The pool and carry.Capture goes to the pool. Long-press to lift, drop to place. "Clone from here" becomes "new line from here".
  3. Gutter dots, peek and "linked from".The first time you'll actually see connections. Peeks that open further peeks.
  4. Station board, and "about" lines with mentions.The phone's map and the best offline feature. Reading a line as one continuous text, plus markdown export, fits here too.
  5. AI orbs from main.Scout first, then the Editor pass over a line.
  6. Tracks view on desktop.The computed map.
  7. Desk canvas.Only if tracks feel too flat. JSON Canvas export.

What I'd park: typed relation schemas, custom-pattern structure (Potluck's lesson), a global graph, and in-thread chat branching (a branch is now just a line sharing stops). Keep customization to choosing views and palettes. Every product that offered deep configuration ended up spending its effort on the settings panel.

Your calls

Four questions only you can answer

  1. Is fiction the main use? If yes, "about" lines, mentions and story time move up. If the audience is thinkers and essayists more broadly, the pool, carry and the Editor pass lead.
  2. Can a thread stop being a chat log? Letting you reorder notes breaks the look of a timestamped feed you add to at the bottom. You could keep that look as an optional view ("as written"). But the default reading of a thread becomes its line order.
  3. Where do you land when you open the app? The pool (Drafts' "text first" model) or the list of lines (today). I lean towards the pool on a phone and lines on desktop.
  4. Should link reasons be encouraged? A one-line "why" on each connection is what makes a web of notes into an argument. It's also friction. My pick is optional, with the field focused and pre-filled.
